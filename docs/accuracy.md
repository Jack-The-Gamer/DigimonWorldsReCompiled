# Accuracy notes

"Exactly as on the original PlayStation" is the bar. This page records what is verified, what is modelled from documentation, and what is still approximated, so nothing is overstated.

## Verified against the games

* All three supported releases boot through the real BIOS (or fast boot), play their FMVs (MDEC + XA audio) and reach gameplay with scripted input; memory cards are detected, written and read.
* They also boot, play and save on the built-in OpenBIOS. Digimon World's new-game save written through OpenBIOS is byte-identical (all 1024 card frames) to the one written through SCPH-5501.
* The recompiler matches the reference interpreter: zero mismatches across ≈224,000 validated block executions (≈30,000 distinct blocks), and pixel-identical frames between engines over minutes of play.
* The other releases boot through their region's console BIOS (fast boot) and through OpenBIOS with zero validation mismatches over 3600 frames each: Digimon World Europe (SLES-02914) and Japan (SLPS-01797) and Digimon World 2 Japan (SLPS-02844) to their intro FMV, Digimon World 3 Japan (SLPS-03446) to its title screen and USA (SLUS-01436) to its load menu (≈241,000 block executions in all). They are recognised but not yet verified end-to-end (gameplay, saves).
* A full BIOS boot needed one fix: on a DICR write, interrupt flags of channels whose enable bit is written as 0 are cleared (Mednafen behaviour). Without it, DMA completion flags left by the BIOS shell raise a spurious interrupt in Digimon World's FMV streaming code.

## Modelled from hardware documentation

The SPU's 4-point interpolation uses the console's exact 512-entry table; a unit test checks the table's defining property (every 4-tap set sums to 0x7F80 ±1).

These follow the nocash PSX specification and Mednafen's hardware-tested behaviour, and match it as far as tested here: CPU pipeline effects, COP0 and exceptions, instruction cache, memory timing, GTE (bit-exact), GPU rasterisation rules, DMA, timers, MDEC decoding, SPU envelopes/ADPCM/reverb, CD-ROM command behaviour, controller and memory-card protocols.

## Options that differ from a console

* **Digimon World 2003 at 60 Hz** (on by default). The European release ships at 50 Hz, about 17% slower than the USA release, but its executable contains a complete 60 Hz mode behind two words of data the game never changes (`0x8005CCAC`, `0x8005CCB0`; see `crates/dwrc-runtime/src/pal60.rs`). Re:Compiled sets them when the executable starts. The game then runs its own NTSC code: the USA release's video timing and display range, and music at the USA tempo (checked by comparing the title music's beat with Digimon World 3 (USA)). A PAL console would do the same with these two values changed, on a TV that accepts 60 Hz. Untick the option for the release as shipped.

* **Widescreen** (Settings → Video, off by default). Games draw their scenes with polygons and sprites that reach past the edges of the screen, and the GPU clips them at the drawing area. With widescreen on, the GPU model draws every primitive that crosses the left or right edge a second time, through the same pixel pipeline, into host-side strips beside the drawing area (`crates/dwrc-psx/src/gpu/wide.rs`), and shows them beside the picture: a 320×240 picture becomes 426×240 for 16:9, with square pixels as before. Nothing is stretched or moved, and the middle of the picture is the console's own, pixel for pixel. An untextured rectangle or screen-aligned quad across the whole width (a backdrop colour, a fade), or such a quad with a texture of a single colour, is drawn across the strips too: Digimon World 2003's battle sky is a flat blue rectangle with cloud pieces over it, and Digimon World 1 fades its scenes with a subtractive quad on a flat texture. The console itself is untouched: VRAM, every register and the game's code run exactly as without it (check mode stays at 0 mismatches with it on), with one exception below. The strips show only what the game sent past the edges. Digimon World 2003's battles and fields and Digimon World 1's fields fill them; Digimon World 2's world map is a 320-pixel picture with nothing beyond. Digimon World 1's fields scroll until the edge of the 4:3 screen meets the end of the map, so a wide picture would show black past it: with widescreen on, the game's own scroll limit (a function at `0x800D7F68` on SLUS-01032, found on other releases by its code) is replaced by one that stops the scroll the strip's width earlier at each end, so the map's ends line up with the edges of the wide picture (a map narrower than the wide picture stays centred). That is the one change to what the game itself does; with widescreen off its own code runs. The strips are cleared when a game starts a new picture in that buffer: the display moving to another buffer, a fill across the whole width, or an upload or copy of at least 8×8 pixels touching the edge; a copy from one screen buffer to another copies the strips with it.

* **Widescreen UI** (with widescreen; off by default). The host builds the whole wide picture itself: every primitive is drawn into the canvas, and uploads, copies and fills are mirrored into it. Pieces of the game's UI, known per game by the texture page and palette (CLUT) row they use, are drawn moved by the strip's width when their group sits within 24 pixels of the left or right edge. Pieces group when they touch and are drawn one after another (unless one lies inside the other, or three quarters of it lies on the other), or touch with only a sliver of overlap; text drawn on a panel moves with it, also a text strip that reaches past its panel's end (Digimon World 1's save slots), while HUD that a window is drawn over keeps its own place; an element counts as at an edge only when it is clearly on that side; a group near no edge that sits right beside a moved one follows it; a group held at one edge that reaches well past the middle (Digimon World 2003's Start menu banner) is stretched: its left half moves left, its right half right, and its middle is drawn in place across the gap. Groups come from the pieces of the last few pictures. A piece no group holds yet (an element growing or sliding in) moves with the group it lies on or touches, a new element with a moved piece beside it in the same picture, and a piece seen before goes back where its group last moved it (the field HUD after a battle or a save state load), so elements appear in place instead of snapping there a few frames later. Areas drawn inside the picture (Digimon World 2003's partner camera) move with their frame. Covered: Digimon World 2003 (battle HUD and every battle menu, Start menu, text boxes), Digimon World 2 (dungeon HUD, battle menus, and the party and enemy panels: each row has three slots, the left and right ones go to the edges and the middle one stays centred), Digimon World 1 (clock, status bars, battle command icon and HP/MP, windows; the black edge of the "BATTLE" banner, made of flat untextured quads, stays with its red letters). Things that float over the scene, like Digimon World 1's enemy HP readout, stay with the scene. Digimon World 2's world map labels share their texture page and palettes with the dungeon walls and stay. Only the picture changes: VRAM and the console are as without it. A screen effect that reads the picture back from VRAM (a battle-start shatter) shows the UI where the game put it.

* **Save states** (through mods such as Save States). A loaded state continues exactly as the game did after it was saved: RAM, VRAM, sound RAM, CPU and every device's registers and timing are restored, and the following frames and audio are bit-identical (tested on all three games, during movies, with zero validation mismatches). Memory cards are deliberately not part of a state, so a game that cached what was on a card before the state was saved may not notice a save written since.

## Known approximations (to be replaced)

| Area | Current state | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| XA-ADPCM resampling coefficients | Same 7-phase × 29-tap structure as the hardware's "zigzag" tables, windowed-sinc coefficients | Very slightly different high-frequency response in streamed music and FMV audio; inaudible in practice, but not bit-exact. |
| MDEC IDCT rounding | nocash formulation with 16-bit-per-pass rounding | Occasional ±1 differences in FMV pixel values. |
| GPU drawing time | Drawing completes instantly (no GPU busy time) | Games are never GPU-bound here, so scenes that slowed down on hardware because of fill rate may run slightly smoother. |
| CD-ROM seek time | Distance-based model (Mednafen formula) | Loading pauses are close to hardware but not identical. |
| Interrupt latency (recompiled engine) | Interrupts are taken between translated blocks (at most one block — up to 96 instructions — later than hardware), also while a block runs on the interpreter waiting for its translation | No observable effect in testing (frames identical to the interpreter). |
| Built-in BIOS (when you have not added your own) | OpenBIOS: an independent implementation of the BIOS kernel and boot loader | Game code is unaffected, but BIOS routines (boot, kernel calls, memory card and CD-ROM library functions) take different amounts of time than Sony's, so timing-sensitive moments can differ slightly. Add a console BIOS for exactness. |
| Controllers | Digital pad (SCPH-1080) | Correct for DW1/DW2/DW3 (they need no analog input). DualShock vibration is not emulated yet. |

## How to check accuracy yourself

* Run any game with **Settings → CPU engine → Validate** to check every translated block against the interpreter while you play (slow).
* `dwrc-cli run <bin> [--bios <bin>] --engine interp|recomp --frames N --shots dir --every K` produces comparable screenshots from either engine.
